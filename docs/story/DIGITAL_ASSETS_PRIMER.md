# Digital Assets Primer (CFA research desk)

Verified facts for Chosen Few Academy scripts. Internal research may use any term; **spoken text always says "digital assets"**. Sources are listed under each section (checked Oct 24, 2026). Not financial or tax advice.

## Bitcoin and blockchain basics  `[basics]`
- Bitcoin is a peer-to-peer electronic cash system described in a whitepaper by the pseudonymous Satoshi Nakamoto, published October 31, 2008. The network launched January 3, 2009 (the genesis block quotes a Times headline about bank bailouts).
- A blockchain is a shared, append-only ledger: transactions are grouped into blocks, each block references the previous one, and thousands of independent computers (nodes) keep and check copies. No single company keeps the ledger.
- Bitcoin adds a block about every 10 minutes on average. Miners compete to add blocks (proof of work); Ethereum uses proof of stake (validators) since September 2022.
- Bitcoin's supply is capped at 21 million coins; new issuance halves about every four years (most recent halving April 2024).
- Different networks (Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana, etc.) are separate ledgers with separate rules. A token on one network is not automatically usable on another; sending on the wrong network can lose funds or need a costly recovery.
- A confirmed transaction cannot be reversed by anyone. There is no chargeback and no bank to call; only the receiver can choose to send funds back.

Sources: https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf · https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/transactions/ · https://ethereum.org/roadmap/merge/

## Wallets: custodial vs self-custody  `[wallets]`
- A wallet does not hold coins. Coins live on the blockchain; a wallet holds the private keys that authorise spending them, and shows balances by reading the ledger.
- Public address = where people send funds (safe to share, like an account number). Private key = what signs transactions (never share).
- Custodial: an exchange or app holds the keys for you (account login, password reset, support desk). Convenient, but you depend on the company: if it fails or freezes withdrawals, you may not get funds back (Mt. Gox 2014, FTX 2022).
- Self-custody: you hold the keys (software 'hot' wallet on a phone/computer, or hardware 'cold' wallet kept offline). No company can freeze it, but no one can reset it for you.
- Hot wallet = keys on an internet-connected device (convenient, more exposed). Cold wallet = keys generated and kept offline, e.g. a hardware device (safer for savings).

Sources: https://ethereum.org/wallets/ · https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-cryptocurrency-scams

## Seed phrases  `[seed]`
- A seed (recovery) phrase is usually 12 or 24 words generated by the wallet. It can regenerate all of that wallet's private keys on any compatible wallet app.
- Anyone who has the seed phrase controls the funds. Losing it (and the device) usually means the funds are gone for good; no company can recover it.
- Store it offline (written on paper or metal, kept somewhere safe like a fireproof box). Never type it into a website, photo, cloud note or chat. No legitimate support agent will ever ask for it.
- Losing the phone is NOT losing the funds if you still have the seed phrase: install a wallet on a new device and restore from the phrase.

Sources: https://help.coinbase.com/en/coinbase/privacy-and-security/avoid-scams/tech-support-scams · https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA240801

## Exchanges and buying for the first time  `[exchanges]`
- An exchange is a company that matches buyers and sellers and usually holds customers' assets (custodial). In the US, regulated exchanges verify identity (KYC) before you can trade.
- Buying on an app gives you a balance on the company's books until you withdraw to your own wallet.
- Exchanges charge trading fees and/or a spread (the gap between buy and sell price); withdrawals add a network fee. Card purchases usually cost more than bank transfers.
- A card decline at an exchange is a bank/card problem (limit, fraud filter, the bank blocking digital asset purchases). Changing wallets, networks or seed phrases does not fix it; using a bank transfer, calling the bank, or waiting for verification does.
- Simple checks before buying: correct official app/URL, account security (2FA), fees shown before confirming, only money you can afford to lose.

Sources: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-alerts/crypto-asset

## Stablecoins  `[stablecoins]`
- A stablecoin is a token designed to track a currency, usually the US dollar, backed by reserves (cash, short-term Treasuries).
- Stable is not guaranteed: a stablecoin can 'de-peg' if reserves or trust fail (e.g., TerraUSD collapsed in May 2022, an algorithmic design without full reserves).
- US GENIUS Act (signed July 18, 2025) requires permitted payment-stablecoin issuers to hold 1:1 reserves in cash, insured deposits, short-term Treasuries and similar, and to publish reserve composition monthly.

Sources: https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1582/text · https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2025/07/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-signs-genius-act-into-law/

## Fees, gas and speed  `[fees]`
- Network fees pay the miners/validators who include transactions. They are not paid to the wallet maker (apps may add their own fee on top).
- Fees rise when the network is busy because block space is limited. A higher fee usually means faster inclusion; a low fee can wait.
- Ethereum 'gas': each transaction pays a base fee (set by the protocol, burned) plus an optional priority fee (tip to the validator). You need a little of the network's native coin (e.g., ETH) to pay gas, even to move a different token.
- Speed differs by network and by confirmations the receiver waits for (exchanges often wait for several confirmations before crediting a deposit).

Sources: https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/gas/ · https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-1559

## Volatility, hype and long-term thinking  `[volatility]`
- Prices move on supply and demand, news, regulation, interest rates and sentiment; with no earnings or central bank behind most tokens, swings of 10%+ in a day happen.
- Bitcoin has had several drawdowns of more than 70% from a peak (e.g., 2018, 2022). Past rises do not predict future returns.
- Hype pressure (FOMO, 'guaranteed' returns, influencer pumps) is a red flag, not a signal.
- Dollar-cost averaging (DCA): buying a fixed dollar amount on a schedule. It smooths the entry price and removes timing pressure; it does not guarantee a profit or prevent losses.
- Only use money you can afford to lose; keep an emergency fund separate.

Sources: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-alerts/crypto-asset

## Scams: phishing, fake support, pig butchering, rug pulls  `[scams]`
- Phishing: fake emails, texts, ads or look-alike sites that steal logins or seed phrases. Type the official URL yourself or use a saved bookmark.
- Fake support: scammers impersonate exchange/wallet support (calls, DMs, search ads). Real support never asks for your seed phrase, passwords, 2FA codes, remote access, or for you to 'move funds to a safe wallet'.
- Pig butchering (relationship investment fraud): a stranger builds trust over weeks, then steers the victim to a fake trading platform that shows fake gains and blocks withdrawals or demands 'taxes/fees' to withdraw. FBI IC3 2024: 41,557 complaints, about $5.8 billion in losses.
- Rug pull: project creators hype a new token then drain its liquidity or abandon it, leaving holders with worthless tokens.
- Giveaway scams: 'send 1, get 2 back' offers, often using a hijacked or fake celebrity/company account, and 'free token' airdrops that ask you to connect a wallet and approve a transaction (which can drain it). Nothing legitimate asks you to pay to receive.
- FBI IC3 2024: about 149,686 complaints referencing these assets with about $9.3 billion in losses; people over 60 lost the most. Report to ic3.gov and reportfraud.ftc.gov.

Sources: https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2024_IC3Report.pdf · https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-cryptocurrency-scams · https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF13008

## Security habits  `[security]`
- Use a unique password and app-based 2FA (not SMS where possible) on exchange and email accounts.
- Before sending: check the full address (first and last characters plus a middle chunk), the network, and send a small test amount first for a new address.
- Address-poisoning and clipboard malware can swap addresses: never copy an address from transaction history.
- Keep a simple record: where each asset is held (which exchange/wallet), how much, and where the backup is stored, without writing the seed phrase in that record.

Sources: https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-cryptocurrency-scams

## US tax basics (not advice)  `[taxes]`
- The IRS treats digital assets as property, not currency (Notice 2014-21). Selling, trading one asset for another, or spending them can create a capital gain or loss.
- Buying with dollars and simply holding is not a taxable event. Moving between your own wallets is not a sale (but keep records).
- Form 1040 asks every filer a yes/no digital asset question. Brokers report sales on Form 1099-DA for transactions from January 1, 2025 (gross proceeds), with cost basis required for many transactions from 2026.
- Good records (date, amount, price paid, fees) are what make the tax math possible.

Sources: https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets · https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-1099-da

## Real history (dates to get right)  `[history]`
- 2008 Oct 31: Bitcoin whitepaper published, weeks after Lehman Brothers' collapse (Sept 15, 2008) in the financial crisis.
- 2009 Jan 3: Bitcoin genesis block mined.
- 2014 Feb: Mt. Gox, then the largest bitcoin exchange (Tokyo), halted withdrawals and filed for bankruptcy, saying about 850,000 bitcoins were missing (around 750,000 of them customers'). Lesson: an exchange holding your keys can fail.
- 2022 Nov 11: FTX filed for bankruptcy after customers rushed to withdraw and the company could not pay; founder Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud in Nov 2023 and sentenced to 25 years in March 2024. Customer funds had been misused.
- 2024 Jan 10: the SEC approved the first US spot bitcoin exchange-traded products (ETFs), letting people get bitcoin price exposure through a regular brokerage account. The SEC stressed this was not an endorsement.

Sources: https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf · https://techcrunch.com/2014/02/28/mt-gox-files-for-bankruptcy/ · https://www.reuters.com/business/ftx-start-us-bankruptcy-proceedings-ceo-exit-2022-11-11/ · https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/gensler-statement-spot-bitcoin-011023

## Story-logic guardrails (the fix must fit the problem)  `[logic]`
- Card declined when buying -> bank/card issue; fix = bank transfer, call the bank, check limits. NOT a new wallet or seed phrase.
- Sent to the wrong address -> usually unrecoverable; fix going forward = test send, check address. Support cannot reverse it.
- Wrong network -> funds may be stuck or lost; sometimes the receiving exchange can recover for a fee, never guaranteed.
- Lost phone with seed phrase backed up -> restore on a new device. Lost seed phrase AND device -> funds unrecoverable.
- Exchange account locked/hacked -> contact official support via the official site, reset passwords/2FA. A seed phrase does not unlock an exchange account.
- Transaction 'pending' -> low fee or busy network; waiting or (on some networks) speeding up with a higher fee helps. Contacting the receiver does not.
- Price dropped -> nothing is 'lost' until sold; no chore (moving wallets) changes the price.
- 'Support' asks for seed phrase -> scam, always.
